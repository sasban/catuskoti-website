Colour

We trust our eyes. We’ve also trained them.

Colour is most of what a coloured gem is worth, and judging it is a craft. A good dealer reads a stone in moments: royal, cornflower, a touch of silk. Yet colour is also one of the hardest things in science to pin down. It shifts with the crystal’s orientation, its inclusions, the cut and the light. We spent careers in industries where colour had to be right every time. That discipline sharpens our eye, and it guides how we choose rough, how we cut it, and how honestly we describe every stone.

The trade’s colours

The names everyone uses. The rules almost nobody reads.

Pigeon’s blood and royal blue are the two most valuable colour names in ruby and sapphire. Yet no lab publishes numbers for them. Each judges by eye against its own master stones, and their definitions differ.

Ruby

Pigeon’s blood

Estimated from GIT’s pigeon’s blood reference stones

A vivid red with no blue or brown modifier, lit from within by red fluorescence: high chromium, low iron.

SSEF · Gübelin: untreated, strong fluorescence. GRS: heated stones can qualify. Gübelin “Crimson Red”: the same colour, with weak fluorescence.

Sapphire

Royal blue

Estimated from SSEF’s royal blue master set

A saturated, vivid blue, pure or with a very slight purplish tint, judged against master stones.

SSEF · Gübelin: untreated stones only. GRS: its own “GRS-type” range; conventional heating not excluded.

Same words, different rules. A heated ruby can be GRS-type “pigeon’s blood”, but never “pigeon blood red” at SSEF or Gübelin.

About

Ratnapura land. A scientist’s eye for colour.

Catuṣkoṭi is a Sri Lankan company built on two strengths: gem-bearing land in the Ratnapura district, held by our family for generations, and a deep understanding of colour.

Our land is mined professionally, by our own teams and by licensed partners. Our colour expertise comes from decades in industries where colour decides whether a product is accepted: thread, textiles and apparel.

We bring that discipline to Ceylon gems, in how we select rough, how we cut it and how we describe every stone. Measuring gem colour precisely remains our long-term research.
